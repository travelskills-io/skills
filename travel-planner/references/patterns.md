# Content patterns

The ten patterns that separate a professional travel planner's writing from amateur or AI-generated content. Read this file for every rewrite or review. Each pattern gives the warning signs, the problem and the correction. The before/after examples, and the phrase lists for each language, are in the examples file of the deliverable's language.

## 1. The encyclopedic travel document

**Warning signs:** a document of more than 20 pages for a one-week trip; general information on the country (history, geography, culture); sections unrelated to the client's trip; decorative photos of places that are not on the program.

**Problem:** the client expects a practical tool, not a personalized guidebook. An overlong document makes the client doubt the planner's ability to sort information, and nobody reads it.

**Correction:** keep what the client uses on the road: day-by-day program, practical information, contacts. Background survives only as one or two lines tied to a place they will actually see.

## 2. Generic destination descriptions

**Warning signs:** brochure clichés (see the list in the examples file); adjectives that would fit any place; superlatives with nothing behind them.

**Problem:** these phrasings say nothing specific and immediately signal brochure or AI copy.

**Correction:** say what makes this place right for this client, with a concrete detail taken from the source or from the planner. When the source holds no facts, write a short text built on what the client told the planner, promise nothing, and list what is missing for the planner.

## 3. An itinerary with no margins or alternatives

**Warning signs:** activities back to back; no travel times between stops; no opening days, booking requirements or entry fees; no plan B.

**Problem:** an overpacked itinerary creates stress instead of removing it.

**Correction:** at least 30 minutes between two major activities; each travel time with its mode of transport; closing days and booking requirements where they matter; a plan B for each day (weather, closure, fatigue).

## 4. Sales pressure instead of advice

**Warning signs:** offer and promotion language, urgency with no reason, "our partner" used as a selling point, calls to book that carry no information (see the list in the examples file).

**Problem:** pressure blurs the advisory relationship and, where the planner does not sell travel, misstates their role and can raise a regulatory issue (France: see `france.md`).

**Correction:** describe the booking in plain terms that match step 1 of `SKILL.md` (who books, how, on what terms), and give the client the information they need to decide.

## 5. One-size-fits-all content

**Warning signs:** "suits every kind of traveller"; no mention of the number of travellers, ages, pace or budget; recommendations that would not change with a different client.

**Problem:** content that could be sent to anyone adds nothing over a web search.

**Correction:** tie each recommendation to something the client said, and let the profile set the pace.

## 6. Tailor-made presented as unaffordable

**Warning signs:** exclusive luxury vocabulary; no price range; implicit elitism.

**Problem:** many prospects believe tailor-made travel is beyond their budget, and this vocabulary confirms it.

**Correction:** give a range, labelled as an estimate, with its assumptions, as step 2 of `SKILL.md` describes for budgets.

## 7. The catalogue proposal

**Warning signs:** a list of services (flights, nights, transfers, excursions); no reference to what the client said; options described by their features, never by what they change for the client; the price before the meaning.

**Problem:** a list of services gets compared line by line, on price alone, with any offer online.

**Correction:** start from the client's wishes and show how each element answers one of them.

## 8. The apologetic fee

**Warning signs:** "my rates are very reasonable", an unrequested discount, a free first call "and then we'll see", the amount slipped in at the end, no list of what is included.

**Problem:** a fee announced with unease invites haggling and makes the client doubt the value of the advice.

**Correction:** state the fee as a fact, with what it covers and what it does not, as step 2 of `SKILL.md` describes.

## 9. The unreadable travel document

**Warning signs:** blocks of text longer than five or six lines; each day laid out differently; practical information buried in descriptions; links missing or dumped in an appendix; a layout designed for print.

**Problem:** the document is read on the road, on a phone. If the client cannot find an address or a time within seconds, they stop using it.

**Correction:** the same short layout for every day (time, place, travel, plan B), one line per item.

## 10. Invented experience and facts

**Warning signs:** a personal experience the user never provided ("I went there last year"); advice attributed to a DMC absent from the notes; prices, hours or closing days stated with no source or date; round figures stated with too much confidence.

**Problem:** a recommendation is worth what its origin is worth. Invented experience misleads the client, and a wrong opening time is discovered in front of a closed door.

**Correction:** cite only the origins the user provides. Facts follow step 3 of `SKILL.md`: verified and dated, or phrased cautiously and listed for the planner.
